GRASP · mechanistic interpretability · one feature, end to end

How a molecular transformer learned to find a phenol

GRASP is a graph transformer pretrained on 1.5 billion molecule presentations. Nobody told it any chemistry. This page follows a single carbon atom from the moment it enters the model to the moment we can point at a number inside the network and say: this means "a carbon carrying an OH group".

Every number on this page comes from the real model. Scroll to begin; drag the 3D view to rotate it.

Part 1 · the chemistry

p-Cresol: one ring, two different jobs

On the right is 4-methylphenol (p-cresol), Cc1ccc(O)cc1, in 3D. It is a benzene ring with a methyl group (CH₃) on one side and a hydroxyl group (OH) on the other.

The two ring carbons holding those groups look alike, but chemistry treats them very differently. The carbon bonded to OH is part of a phenol: the O–H hydrogen bonds to water, is mildly acidic (pKa about 10), pushes electron density into the ring, and lowers logP. The carbon bonded to CH₃ does none of that.

A model that "knows chemistry" should be able to tell these two carbons apart.

Part 2 · the AI · what the model is given

Each atom becomes a token, and the twins get the same one

GRASP does not see a picture or coordinates. Each heavy atom is replaced by one token, a short ID describing only the atom itself: element, number of bonds, number of hydrogens, charge, ring or not (a radius-0 Morgan invariant). Hydrogens are folded into their atom.

The two highlighted carbons
C–OH carbon → token 150 (C, 3 bonds, 0 H, in ring)
C–CH₃ carbon → token 150 (C, 3 bonds, 0 H, in ring)

Same token. Whatever difference GRASP ends up with must come from the neighbours. The model also gets one more input: the number of bonds between every pair of atoms, which it uses inside attention.

Part 2 · the AI · from token to vector

A token becomes a list of 768 numbers

The token is looked up in a table and turned into a vector of 768 numbers. On the right, the 768 numbers of the C–OH carbon are laid out as a 24 × 32 grid: height and colour show each value (real values, layer 0).

This vector is the atom's residual stream, its running notes. Each of GRASP's 12 layers reads every atom's vector and adds an update to it.

One footnote: the released GRASP also adds an atom-order tag (an absolute position embedding), so the twins' vectors already differ a little at layer 0. GRASP-A1, which drops that tag, starts the twins out identical and still learns the same features. The tag carries no chemistry.

Part 2 · the AI · attention along bonds

Each layer, the atom listens to its neighbours

In attention, every atom decides how much to read from every other atom. GRASP's attention is told how many bonds apart the two atoms are. The lines on the right show where the C–OH carbon looks at each layer (thicker = more attention, averaged over its 12 heads).

At layer 3 the trained model puts 67% of this carbon's attention on its three bonded neighbours, and the oxygen gets the largest single share. The untrained copy spreads attention evenly, about 34% on neighbours at every layer, which is roughly what you get by chance.

Part 2 · the AI · the twins drift apart, then together

By layer 6, the twins carry different notes

On the right are the layer-6 vectors of both twin carbons side by side. The chart tracks how similar they are at every layer (cosine similarity; 1 = identical direction).

In GRASP-A1 the twins start identical (1.00) and are pushed apart as neighbour information arrives. Late layers squeeze everything back together (0.99 at layer 12). That collapse is the "layer-12 blur" we are investigating separately. The useful chemistry lives in the middle layers.

Part 3 · the mechanism · the reading problem

768 numbers, but which ones mean "OH"?

Here are 1,528 real token-150 carbons from 4,000 drug-like molecules, laid out by the two main directions of their layer-6 vectors. The 128 carbons that carry an OH are mixed in with the others.

No single one of the 768 numbers means "OH". Networks store far more concepts than they have dimensions, by overlapping them (superposition). Each number takes part in many concepts, so reading neurons one at a time does not work.

Part 3 · the mechanism · the sparse autoencoder

Split the vector into thousands of rarely-used parts

A sparse autoencoder (SAE) is a second, small network trained to rewrite each 768-number vector as a combination of a large dictionary of directions, using only a few at a time.

What it computes
encode: z = TopK₃₂( W_enc · x + b ) → 4,096 slots, 32 non-zero
decode: x̂ = Σ zⱼ · dⱼ → rebuild the 768 numbers
train: make x̂ ≈ x on 1,157,595 atoms

On the right, the C–OH carbon's vector (left grid) goes in, the 4,096 dictionary slots (middle) are tested, and only the 32 strongest are kept. Those 32 directions are added back up to rebuild the vector (right grid). For this atom the rebuilt vector points almost the same way as the original (cosine 0.93); across all 1.16 million training atoms the SAE explains 92.5% of the variance.

Forcing sparsity is the trick. With only 32 of 4,096 slots available per atom, each slot tends to specialise in one recognisable pattern, and that is what makes it readable.

Part 3 · the mechanism · which slots fire

The twins share some slots, and one slot is the OH carbon's alone

Of the 32 slots that fire on the C–OH carbon, 11 also fire on its twin. They encode what the carbons have in common (aromatic ring carbon, substituted, para to another group).

Slot #2610 is the second-strongest on the C–OH carbon (value 6.83) and does not fire at all on the twin. The question is whether it actually means "carbon carrying OH" or just happened to fire here.

Part 3 · the mechanism · testing what a feature means

The same-token test

To test slot 2610 fairly, look only at atoms with the same input token (150), so the token cannot be giving the answer, and ask whether the slot separates OH-carbons from the rest. On the right, every carbon rises by its slot value.

Of 15,962 token-150 carbons without OH, 15,915 read exactly 0. All 128 of 128 OH-carbons fire. Measured as a classifier, that is an AUROC of 0.99999. The untrained copy's best slot fires on 98% of OH-carbons, but also on 13% of everything else. It reacts to the whole molecule containing an O–H token somewhere, not to the right carbon.

The input tokens are identical for both networks, so the difference comes from what GRASP learned during pretraining. To detect the phenol it had to combine information along bonds, exactly as a chemist does.

What this shows is correlation inside the model: the feature reliably encodes the phenol. Whether later layers actually use it is the next experiment (removing the feature and watching what breaks), which is running now.
