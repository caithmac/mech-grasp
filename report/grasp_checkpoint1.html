GRASP · mechanistic interpretability · checkpoints 1–4

Does GRASP know chemistry?

Yes, and we can now see how. GRASP passes information along bonds like a message-passing network, builds features that recognise functional groups in context (none exist in an untrained copy), and notices when a molecule's connectivity is scrambled. That chemistry comes from self-supervised pretraining, not from the assay labels. The assay step adds a real but narrow toxicity signal. The functional-group features are causally used: removing one at layer 6 takes its concept away from later layers, and for polar groups it moves the logP read-out in the chemically expected direction. The final layer collapses into a few directions and discards fine-grained chemistry in every checkpoint, including GRASP-A2 with explicit bond and stereo inputs. The released model's dependence on SMILES order is caused by its absolute position embeddings: GRASP-A1, trained without them, is exactly order-invariant and loses nothing. GRASP-A3, which replaces the hashed atom token with seven explicit atom fields, builds the most context-chemistry features of the four checkpoints and uses them causally. It is also the first that reads stereochemistry noticeably.

Verdict
Chemistry

Layers 3–8 compute local chemistry

On atoms whose token cannot decide, GRASP's errors are 11× to over 1,000× lower than a random-init control. The receptive field grows with depth: neighbours by layer 4, rings by layers 6–8.

Chemistry

Its features are functional groups in context

A sparse autoencoder on layer 6 finds 93 features that split atoms sharing one input token by chemistry (phenol C–OH, carboxylate C, sulfonamide N, CF₃ carbon; AUROC up to 1.00). The same analysis on a random-init network finds 0.

Causal

The features are used, not just present

Removing one layer-6 chemistry feature removes 3–32% of its concept from later layers (more when its duplicates go too); an equal-size random edit removes nothing. Removing the carboxylate, phenol or sulfonamide feature raises predicted logP in 92–100% of molecules, as chemistry predicts.

Mechanism

Information flows along bonds

Neighbour-attending heads sit in layers 1–4. A single-atom edit spreads outward about one bond per one to two layers; in a random network it spreads evenly with no regard to distance.

Chemistry

It notices scrambled connectivity

Swap two bonds so every atom token is unchanged: GRASP separates original from rewired molecules with AUROC 0.78 (A1: 0.83); random-init 0.57, token counts 0.50.

Source

Pretraining, not assays, taught the chemistry

GRASP-A1 before assay supervision (Step 1) matches the final model on every atom-level probe. The assay step adds only a toxicity signal.

Toxicity

Real, narrow, and possibly leaked

Four Tox21 stress-response endpoints beat every control by 0.05–0.11 AUROC, and that gain appears only after Step-2 assay training, whose ChEMBL data may contain Tox21. Nuclear-receptor endpoints and ClinTox show no learned signal.

Shortcut

SMILES order leaks in through position embeddings

Rewriting a SMILES string moves a layer-12 logP prediction by 0.57 on average. GRASP-A1, the same recipe without absolute positions, is exactly order-invariant and learns the same chemistry.

Lost

Layer 12 collapses and discards chemistry

In all four checkpoints the atom representation shrinks from ~180 effective dimensions at layer 8 to 4–22 at layer 12. Even a non-linear probe then reads atom logP contributions no better than from a random network. This comes from pretraining, not the assays. Lipinski flags are no test at all: token counts predict them as well as GRASP.

GRASP-A3

Explicit atom fields: the most chemistry, and it is used

A3's input is element, degree, H count, charge, aromaticity, ring membership and hybridization. On top of that it learns ring size, conjugation and atom logP contributions better than A2, has 86 features that split atoms with identical inputs (GRASP 75, A1 70, A2 66; untrained A3: 3), and removing one cuts its concept downstream by up to 37%. It is also 16× more stereo-sensitive than A2. Its last layer still collapses.

GRASP-A2

Explicit bonds sharpen chemistry, but don't change the story

A2 learns aromaticity ~5× more precisely than A1 and builds about as many context features as GRASP (72 vs 81 on equally trained dictionaries), but its random control gains almost nothing from the bond inputs, and its last layer collapses like the others. Stereo inputs reach the model and spread outward along bonds, but change the final embedding by only ~0.3%, and diastereomers move less than enantiomers.

Additivity test

4 · Similarity geometry

GRASP's idea of "similar" is not fingerprint overlap

Over 3,000 random molecule pairs, we rank-correlated embedding distance with Morgan Tanimoto dissimilarity. Spearman ρ: GRASP CLS layer 12 0.12, layer 6 0.38, mean-pooled layer 6 0.37; random-init network 0.55 at every layer. Pretraining reorganises molecular similarity away from shared substructure, most strongly at the final layer. That is expected for a model tuned on assay labels, but it means nearest-neighbour searches in GRASP space will not behave like fingerprint searches.

Leakage caveat

GRASP's Step-2 set is built from ChEMBL activities, and the build removed only TDC ADMET test-set molecules. ChEMBL hosts Tox21 depositions, and the 642 assays are stored by internal ChEMBL assay IDs that we could not map offline. Do the probes look at toxicophores? A linear probe on a mean pool splits exactly into per-atom contributions. For positive test molecules containing a classic toxicophore (nitroaromatic, aromatic amine, Michael acceptor, polycyclic aromatic, epoxide and others), we checked whether the top-contributing atom lies inside it. 11 · Lipinski's rule of 5

Rule-of-5 flags test atom counting, not chemistry

On 8,622 Tox21 and ClinTox molecules (7.7% over 500 Da, 9.3% logP over 5, 5.8% failing the rule), every Ro5 flag is linearly readable from GRASP with AUROC 0.98–0.999. So it is from a bag of input tokens (0.991–0.9998) and from the random-init network (0.989–0.9998), because molecular weight, donor and acceptor counts are atom counts and logP is additive. Thresholding a continuous logP probe at 5 predicts the flag at least as well as a probe trained on the flag itself (0.997 vs 0.990), so there is no special rule-threshold representation. Task T11.

Three things follow. The features are causal: equal-size random edits do nothing. No single feature carries a whole concept: duplicates and the part of the activation the SAE does not capture hold the rest, and steering moves read-outs toward a concept without ever flipping an atom. And the effect fades to 0–0.10 by layer 12, consistent with the layer-12 collapse below. Where the logP read-out does move, it moves the chemically correct way for polar groups; it does not respond consistently to the halogen, ester, ether-O or aliphatic-OH features.

13 · Why layer 12 blurs chemistry

GRASP's Step-1 objective is replaced-token detection (RTD): a small generator swaps 25% of atom tokens and the 12-layer encoder must say, for every atom, whether its token was replaced; the RTD head reads layer 12. The simple explanation, that the last layer is where replacements are detected, is wrong: detection is essentially complete by layers 2–4. What happens at layers 10–12 is a collapse: atoms are squeezed into a handful of directions (anisotropy up to 0.8), and quantitative chemistry is discarded, not just hidden. At layer 12 even a non-linear probe reads atom logP contributions no better than from a random network, while coarse categories (aromatic or not, next to O or not) survive. The collapse is strongest in A1 Step 1 (RTD only, 3.8 effective dimensions) and partly undone by assay training. It appears in every checkpoint, including A2. Our working hypothesis, not yet tested, is that the final layers are shaped to feed the single-logit RTD head, for which "does this token fit here?" matters and the fine detail does not.

Practical consequence

The README's default representation, the final-layer CLS embedding, reads from the layer where atom-level chemistry is weakest. For chemistry-sensitive tasks use layers 4–8 (GRASP's fine-tuning recipe already mixes layers 4/8/10/12, which helps).

15 · GRASP-A3

Seven explicit atom fields: more chemistry on top, and it is causal

GRASP-A3 keeps A2's bond and stereo inputs but replaces the hashed atom token with seven categorical fields: element, degree, total H, formal charge, aromatic, in ring, hybridization. Its replaced-token objective also predicts those seven fields. The input is therefore finer than GRASP's token: 75 distinct input bundles on our atoms against 57 tokens, each bundle inside one token. Recommendations for GRASP users
  • Canonicalise SMILES (Chem.MolToSmiles(Chem.MolFromSmiles(s))) before calling encode(); consider doing it inside encode().
  • Prefer GRASP-A1 where it performs comparably: it is exactly order-invariant and carries the same atom-level chemistry.
  • For property prediction from frozen embeddings, use mean-pooled atom vectors from layers 4–8 rather than the final-layer CLS: layer 12 has discarded most atom-level chemistry in every checkpoint (section 13).
  • If you need atom-level chemistry from a frozen encoder, GRASP-A3 carries the most of it. If you need enantiomers to get identical predictions for achiral properties, check A3's outputs: they now differ slightly.
  • Treat the layer-12 CLS as assay-specialised: good for transfer tasks that resemble Step-2 assays, weaker as a general chemistry representation.
  • Before claiming toxicity transfer, check the target assays against the 642 Step-2 ChEMBL assays; publishing their ChEMBL IDs with the model would make this easy.
How this was done

Local agents did the work; every claim was audited

A Python harness drives four local agents through Ollama: an MI engineer that writes and runs experiment scripts against a fixed library with built-in controls, a chemist that interprets results, a skeptical critic, and a scribe. The supervisor (Claude) wrote the task specs and the verified library (probes, interventions, sparse autoencoder), intervened through hint files, and re-checked every claim before it entered the ledger. Checkpoint 2 ran 11 tasks in about 7 hours on one 16 GB GPU, with a 35B mixture-of-experts model (3B active) at about 60 tokens/s.

181 claims in ledger123 confirmed (20 after correction)24 rejected19 weak, untested or plausible15 process notes or superseded
  • The agent pipeline found the order-sensitivity first (T00), with the wrong magnitude; the supervisor confirmed and measured it.
  • Agents' numbers were wrong in 4 places (summed norms, token counting, a misaligned drift estimate, a broken random control). The critic caught three of them.
  • Two label bugs were the supervisor's own: deprotonated acids missing from the carboxylic-acid SMARTS, and Crippen hydrogens missing from atom contributions. Both were caught by checks and fixed before the claims that depend on them.
  • Small single-purpose tasks work with a 9B model; long multi-part specs fail repeatedly. Tasks T03, T04, T05 and T06 were split accordingly.
  • Checkpoint 2: the agents ran every experiment, but in 3 of 11 tasks (T12, T07a, T10b) their final numbers were wrong, and in 2 more (T09, T05b) an earlier run or a bookkeeping field was. Causes: reading the wrong result keys, a row offset that never advanced, misses never counted, an exception hiding every probe. Because they used a library that caches intermediate results, the supervisor could re-extract or recompute the correct numbers. Three of those bugs led to harness changes: results with no finite numbers, or only error entries, now fail the run.
  • The critic caught most broken runs, but also endorsed two chemically wrong readings (Tox21 SR-MMP taken as matrix metalloproteinase rather than mitochondrial membrane potential; "aromaticity is in the token") and once rejected a correct result over a bookkeeping field. Supervisor review stayed necessary.
  • Checkpoint 3 (8 tasks): the causal-ablation task failed twice on row bookkeeping (10 attempts), so the supervisor added a tested helper (mechlib.edits) that does the mapping; with it the agents completed the task, but one condition was still wrong and was recomputed. Recurring failure: a per-item try/except that swallows every error and writes an empty or partial result. The critic caught a hard-coded 0.0 standing in for a measurement (T15b) and the chemist repeatedly cited metrics that did not exist; those claims were rejected.
  • Checkpoint 4 (GRASP-A3, 4 tasks): the agents completed the SAE and collapse tasks, and both reproduced exactly. They failed the stereo task 5 times, and their probe run collected no data (the critic rejected it). The supervisor ran both from the accepted A2 scripts. A re-audit with one tested counting routine found the A2 count of 178 context features was wrong (72; latents grouped by element, not token). The supervisor also briefly "corrected" GRASP's 93 to 81 before noticing the two numbers come from different dictionaries; 93 stands.
Open questions
  • Toxicity leakage: map the 642 Step-2 ChEMBL assay IDs to descriptions and check for Tox21 depositions. This decides whether the Step-2 stress-response gain is learned biology or memorised assays.
  • Test the RTD-head explanation of the layer-12 collapse directly: with the released RTD head, or intermediate Step-1 checkpoints, check whether the collapsed directions are the ones the head reads.
  • A2 stereo is barely used. Test it where stereo matters: stereo-specific activity cliffs in ChEMBL, or fine-tuning on a chiral endpoint.
  • Causal role of the SAE features in the toxicity read-outs (section 12 covered logP only).
  • The 182 constant-label Step-2 assays (4.4% of labels): test whether they taught chemical-series membership rather than activity.
  • Ames and hERG were not tested (no offline copies); MoleculeNet Tox21 and ClinTox were used instead.
